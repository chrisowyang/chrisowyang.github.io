*Working notes — opinions mine, examples deliberately generic.*

The uncomfortable truth about subscriptions is that the business model
contains a temptation: once someone subscribes, inertia does part of your
job. Plenty of subscription businesses quietly optimize for the moment of
forgetting — the gym-membership model, applied to software.

I think that's both wrong and, increasingly, bad strategy. My working rule:
**a subscription owes its subscriber a monthly answer to "what did you do
for me lately?"** — and the product, not the marketing team, should be the
one answering.

A few things I believe after working on this at scale:

1. **Retention you'd be embarrassed to explain isn't retention.** If your
   churn model's best feature is "hasn't opened the app in 60 days," you
   don't have a retention strategy, you have a countdown.
2. **The best subscriptions adapt.** People's lives are lumpy — travel,
   holidays, tight months. A plan that flexes with how someone actually
   lives earns a kind of loyalty a static discount never will.
3. **Cancellation is a product surface, not a leak.** How you treat someone
   on the way out is the strongest signal of whether they'll come back.
   Most companies treat it as a hostage negotiation.

The through-line to the rest of my work: this is a personalization problem.
A subscription is a standing relationship, and relationships that don't
adapt to the other person don't last.
